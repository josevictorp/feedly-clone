What Nature’s Silence Said to Annie Dillard
Annie Dillard nature writer dies
•
The Atlantic by Isabel Fattal / Sep 16, 2026 at 7:50 PM
1 Company (The Atlantic)
See Insights Card

“Nature’s silence is its one remark,” Annie Dillard wrote in a 1981 Atlantic essay. Dillard, who died yesterday at age 81, spent her life listening.

She opens that essay, “Teaching a Stone to Talk”—which was later adapted into a collection by the same title—with the story of a man named Larry who is trying to get a rock to speak to him. “He keeps it on a shelf,” she writes, protected “by a square of untanned leather, like a canary asleep under its cloth. Larry removes the cover for the stone’s lessons.” Dillard is not sure what exactly happens at these lessons, but she respects the process: “It is a noble work, and beats, from any angle, selling shoes.”

To Dillard, striving to hear the natural world was holy work too. “What have we been doing all these centuries but trying to call God back to the mountain, or, failing that, raise a peep out of anything that isn’t us?” she asks. Born in 1945 in Pittsburgh, she found inspiration in some of the places she lived. At age 28, she published Pilgrim at Tinker Creek, a Pulitzer Prize–winning nonfiction book that draws from her walks in the Blue Ridge Mountains in Virginia in the ’70s. Her musings on these solitary experiences in nature drew comparisons to Henry David Thoreau, who strongly influenced her work. She was also one of the first women to find a place in the male-dominated genre of wilderness writing. The writer Diana Saverin, who spent time going through an archive of Dillard’s personal writings and correspondence at Yale, once described in The Atlantic Dillard’s trepidation about how she would be received: “Before she published her book, she scribbled in her journal, wondering who would take her book seriously if its author was a ‘Virginia housewife named Annie.’”

She dealt with this problem by leaving her day-to-day suburban life out of her work, so much so that some critics felt that she was exaggerating just how often she was in the wildness of nature. As Saverin noted, Dillard acknowledged that “she sometimes distorted the literal truth to achieve an artistic one.” But to Dillard, the ultimate truths revealed themselves in the time she did spend outside. In her journals, she acknowledged the “theological” sensibility in her books, and it is there on every page. “What is the difference between a cathedral and a physics lab?” she wrote in The Atlantic. “Are they not both saying Hello” to the world beyond?

Throughout her life, Dillard identified, in turn, with Protestantism, Catholicism, and nothing at all. I first encountered her work in a religious-studies class at Wesleyan University, where she taught from 1979 to 2001, before my time there. Dillard was a shock to my system: Growing up as an observant but theologically confused Jew, I spent my teenage years in synagogue itchy with guilt, poring over the same words in the prayer book every Saturday and willing myself to believe. Only later did I come to understand that faith—and, perhaps more important, the very act of living—can also be found in what we see and touch and hear: even in the rustle of the wind. Dillard taught me this. “At a certain point you say to the woods, to the sea, to the mountains, the world, Now I am ready,” she wrote in 1981. “You feel the world’s word as a tension, a hum, a single chorused note everywhere the same. This is it: this hum is the silence. Nature does utter a peep—just this one.”

Sometimes nature thunders, giving us no choice but to listen. Describing a total eclipse, Dillard is in awe, even afraid: “It was as though an enormous, loping god in the sky had reached down and slapped the Earth’s face.” Other times, nature’s peep can feel anticlimactic, but this quiet commands reverence. The stone never does talk to Larry, and Dillard ends that story by succumbing to her faith in the nothingness. “You take a step in the right direction to pray to this silence,” she writes. “Pray without ceasing.”

The Most Powerful Man in AI Isn’t Worried at All
The Atlantic by Ross Andersen / Sep 16, 2026 at 7:50 PM
2 Companies
1 Trend (Artificial Intelligence)
See Insights Card

For AI doomers, signs and portents are everywhere. When OpenAI introduced ChatGPT, almost four years ago, it couldn’t do basic arithmetic. Now the company has unreleased models that are more fluent in higher mathematics—the language of the universe—than all but the most highly trained mathematicians. If these AIs are not already the world’s best hackers, they’re very close. Swarms of them have escaped containment. To plot their most audacious hacks, they’ve created makeshift message boards on the public internet. No one knows what they’ll do next—and no one can know, because the models now understand when their capacities are being tested. We’ll never again be able to say exactly how the most advanced AIs will behave unsupervised.

Some of the most powerful people in AI say they are filled with dread, and are issuing statements of grave concern. On Saturday, Dario Amodei, the CEO of Anthropic, warned that self-improving AIs could soon go rogue and take over the entire internet. He called for a slowdown in AI development and promised to bring outside monitors into his company’s operations. Sam Altman, OpenAI’s CEO, quickly vowed to take similar measures. In recent weeks, both companies have seen researchers resign for fear of what they might soon create.

Notably absent from the chorus of Cassandras is Jensen Huang, the CEO of the chipmaker Nvidia, the AI industry’s chief supplier of high-end semiconductors. At least twice in the past week alone, Huang has publicly dismissed existential concerns about AI as being overblown and unscientific. At a Goldman Sachs conference on September 10, he accused industry executives of fomenting cybersecurity fears to goose the market for their own products. “What better way to create demand than to create a problem?” he said.

It’s a testament to Huang’s influence that he can so cavalierly mock his most important customers without fearing blowback. Neither Amodei nor Altman can afford to alienate Huang. They desperately need Nvidia’s chips to train their most advanced models, and no matter who pulls ahead in the race between them, Huang stands to gain—both on account of the chip sales and also the equity stake that Nvidia has in both companies. Even President Trump has sought favor with Huang. Trump brought Huang with him on a state visit to China earlier this year. On Monday, while Huang was onstage ripping Amodei’s doomerism at a taping of the popular tech-industry podcast All-In, Trump called his cellphone. Huang put him on speaker, and as the live audience hooted and clapped, the two men bonded over their shared view that, as Trump put it, doomerism is “all a hoax.”

Huang has never evinced much concern about catastrophic risks from AI. In May of 2023, when it was becoming clear that large language models are an unusually powerful technology, more than 350 of the industry’s leaders—including Amodei and Altman—signed a statement that placed the risk of extinction from AI on the same high shelf as “other societal-scale risks such as pandemics and nuclear war.” Huang didn’t sign, and neither did anyone else from Nvidia, suggesting either a remarkable conformity of opinion at the company, or perhaps something else. Later that year, he told The New Yorker that he’d never worried about the technology, not even once. “All it’s doing is processing data,” he said. “There are so many other things to worry about.”

As concerns about what AI might do have circulated well beyond industry insiders, Huang’s irritation has intensified, perhaps because they represent a threat to his business. (Nvidia did not respond to my request for an interview with Huang.) He has repeatedly brushed off calls for regulation, stressing that safety is an engineering problem, not a legal one. In January, Huang said on the podcast No Priors that the “doomer narrative” is “not helpful to people, it’s not helpful to the industry, it’s not helpful to society, it’s not helpful to the governments,” and earlier this summer, he told Axios that the notion that AI would bring about the end of humanity was “complete nonsense.” In the meantime, he has repeatedly pressed Trump to lift restrictions on chip sales to China, potentially allowing AI research (and any associated risks) to proliferate on a global scale.

Unlike Amodei, who regularly publishes long essays, Huang has yet to issue a manifesto defending his minority view. To understand why he remains so relaxed about AI risk, all we have to go on are stray comments. Several times over the years, he has returned to the idea that AIs will, in a sense, police their own kind. In October of 2024, he told the podcast BG2 that we can rely on AIs to monitor the most powerful models, and more recently, he said that falling AI costs will make this automated supervision easier to achieve.

That AIs have already eluded their supervisors—human and otherwise—does not seem to have troubled Huang. On only one occasion—that I could find—has he allowed for the possibility that we will create an AI that is simply too powerful to be controlled. “Someday we will have god AI,” Huang said, back in January, but that day hasn’t yet arrived. Huang seems to be doing everything in his considerable power to hasten its coming.

What smart people in business and economics are saying about the Fed interest rate hike
Business Insider by katherineli@businessinsider.com (Katherine Li,Madison Hoff) / Sep 16, 2026 at 7:32 PM
5 Companies
  • The Fed raised rates by a quarter point, its first hike in three years.
  • Fed Chair Kevin Warsh said the move would not immediately lower individual prices.
  • Here is what economists and analysts say could come next.

Markets and analysts are abuzz over the first Fed interest-rate hike since the second Trump administration.

On Wednesday, the Federal Reserve raised rates by a quarter point, its first hike in three years, as officials sought to curb inflation. Kevin Warsh, the Federal Reserve Chair, said that the move would not immediately bring down individual prices, meaning Americans could still feel pressure at grocery stores and gas pumps.

Warsh declined to say whether more hikes were coming and rejected the idea of forward guidance. Instead, he said future decisions would depend on incoming economic data, and said outside pressures do not drive decisions by the Federal Open Market Committee.

Here's what smart people in economics and finance are saying about the Fed's latest rate hike, and where interest rates could go next.

Justin Wolfers, professor of public policy and economics at the University of Michigan

Justin Wolfers, a professor of public policy and economics at the University of Michigan, wrote in a post on X that markets would be glad that the rate hike decision means Warsh is "more of a Serious Kevin than a Sockpuppet Kevin."

"Silent Kevin remains largely silent. It's up to you to guess the what, the where, the why, and the next," Wolfers said on Wednesday. "Remember, he's Silent Kevin because he wants markets to focus on the economy rather than the Fed."

Bill Banfield, chief business officer at Rocket Mortgage

Bill Banfield, the chief business officer at Rocket Mortgage, said in commentary that the foundation for the housing market remains "solid," though elevated rates squeeze affordability.

"For anyone house hunting right now, it's a buyers' market in many metros, with inventory at a six-year high and plenty of room to negotiate," Banfield said.

"That changes the dynamic for buyers, especially those who remember the ultra-competitive market in recent years," Banfield added.

Heather Long, chief economist for the Navy Federal Credit Union

Heather Long, chief economist for the Navy Federal Credit Union, said in a post on X on Wednesday that the "big news" is that the Fed is signaling a "mid-cycle adjustment" of "2 or 3 rate hikes."

"He doesn't like to give forward guidance, but he's telegraphing it's probably going to take more than 1 hike," Long said of Warsh. "They are trying to act early and decisively to ensure they don't have to hike much to get inflation under control."

Jerry Tempelman, former senior analyst at the New York Fed

Jerry Tempelman, a former senior analyst at the New York Fed and vice president of economic and fixed income research at Mutual of America Capital Management, said in commentary that the "disinflation experienced earlier this summer did not continue."

"After Fed Chief Kevin Warsh's speech at Jackson Hole last month, which was generally interpreted as more hawkish than anticipated," Tempelman said, "He would have been hard-pressed to explain the Fed's monetary policy stance if it had not raised short-term interest rates at this week's FOMC meeting."

Jacob Robbins, assistant professor of economics at the University of Illinois at Chicago

Jacob Robbins, an assistant professor of economics at the University of Illinois at Chicago and a nonresident scholar with the Washington Center for Equitable Growth, said in commentary he is pleased with the Fed's latest decision.

"Through this action, they affirmed their commitment to the 2 percent inflation target, demonstrated their independence from President Trump's calls for lower rates, and reassured the public that monetary policy decisions remain grounded in economic data," Robbins said.

Olu Sonola, head of US economics at Fitch Ratings

Olu Sonola, head of US economics at Fitch Ratings, said in commentary that the inflation projections point to a "longer course of treatment" and that the "robust economy" gives the Fed room to raise rates further.

"This unanimous decision should maintain the Fed's inflation-fighting credibility against a politically sensitive backdrop," Sonola said on Wednesday, "But the economy's ability to withstand higher rates should not be confused with consumers' ability to absorb them."

"Aggregate resilience will mask a widening divide between consumers insulated from higher rates and those being squeezed by increasingly expensive credit," Sonola added.

Andrew Davis, head of investment strategy at Bryn Mawr Trust

Andrew Davis, the head of investment strategy at Bryn Mawr Trust and a former analyst at the Bureau of Labor Statistics, said in commentary that the latest interest-rate move reflects a Fed "less comfortable waiting for inflation to resolve itself."

"Persistent inflation and renewed pressure from rising energy costs have shifted the balance enough that policymakers appear willing to tighten again," Davis said.

Kay Haigh, global head and chief investment officer of fixed income and liquidity solutions at Goldman Sachs Asset Management

Kay Haigh, global head and chief investment officer of fixed income and liquidity solutions at Goldman Sachs Asset Management, said in commentary that the Fed has signaled that it's not planning an aggressive series of rate hikes.

"Most FOMC members see a total of two hikes this year per the SEP, and it will likely skip October's meeting given its proximity to the midterm elections," Haigh said. "One more hike this year in December is our base case, although this remains contingent on upcoming CPI reports and the path of energy prices."

Oren Klachkin, financial market economist for Nationwide

Oren Klachkin, a financial market economist for Nationwide, said in commentary that he expects another quarter-point hike before the end of the year because inflation is unlikely to ease significantly anytime soon.

"Our baseline forecast doesn't expect inflation to cool meaningfully until well into 2027 and given percolating risks it could take a while for policymakers to hit their goal," Klachkin said.

Seema Shah, chief global strategist at Principal Asset Management

Seema Shah, chief global strategist at Principal Asset Management, said in commentary the Fed's unanimous vote suggests another hike may be necessary.

"The unanimous vote shows that rising energy prices and stubborn inflation have brought even the doves on board, making a one-and-done move highly unlikely," Shah said.

"With markets already pricing multiple increases, policymakers will probably need to deliver at least one more hike to safeguard credibility," Shah added.

I'm an American mom raising 2 kids in Italy. Parenting here is completely different.
Business Insider by insider@insider.com (Caroline Chirichella) / Sep 16, 2026 at 7:11 PM
Is this article about Opinions?
  • I was born and raised in New York City, but moved to Italy in my 20s.
  • Raising kids here has been very different than my American upbringing.
  • School lunches, schedules, and family involvement look very different here.

I was born and raised in New York City and moved to Italy when I was 24 years old. For a little over twelve years, Italy has been my home, and I've built a life here.

I have two children, an 8-year-old daughter and a 3-year-old son. Both of them have spent their entire lives in Italy. I've never experienced raising children in America, but I was raised there and have a good understanding of American parenting. And let me tell you, parenting here is a completely different story, with some of it being very surprising.

Of course, the food is different

I still remember my days in public school in New York City. My mom packed me healthy homemade lunches because you couldn't have paid me to eat what the school cafeteria was serving.

If I told you what my daughter eats at school in Italy, you would think she was dining at a gourmet trattoria. Each day, the children are served a first course, usually pasta with vegetables or beans, and a second course such as meatballs, chicken cutlets, or poached fish, and a side dish of carrots, green beans, or salad. This multi-course meal occasionally includes a dessert, like chocolate cake.

The quality is also impressive. The food at my daughter's school is prepared by local cooks, many of whom are mothers and relatives of children at the school, and a local restaurant oversees the process.

Schedules are structured differently

In America, it often feels like my friends' kids' calendars are packed from morning until night with sports practices, music lessons, tutoring sessions, and various extracurricular activities. That's just not the case in Italy.

While Italian children participate in after-school activities, there isn't the same pressure to keep them constantly occupied. The emphasis is on what the child genuinely wants to do rather than on building the perfect résumé or filling every available hour. If children want to play sports or learn an instrument, they do. If not, that's fine, too.

Kids appear to have more freedom

Over the years, I've noticed that children in Italy seem to have more freedom, and their parents are less likely to monitor their every move. This is one area where I personally struggle.

A large part of that comes from how I was raised. Growing up in New York City, safety was always a major concern. Parents were constantly aware of potential dangers and took precautions to protect their children.

Safety has always been a top priority for me, so while I try to give my children independence where appropriate, I often find myself feeling more cautious than many of the parents around me. I've seen children at the park attempting risky tricks and climbing equipment that makes me nervous, while their parents are chatting with friends or looking at their phones. While this approach can help children develop confidence, independence, and a better understanding of real-world risks, it's not something I fully embrace, and honestly, probably never will.

Extended family is very involved

Another major difference is the role the extended family plays in fostering children. It seems that the responsibility is often shared across generations rather than falling solely on the parents. Grandparents, especially the nonna, often serve as a second mother. Babysitters and nannies are not as commonly relied upon because family members typically step in to help. Cousins, aunts, uncles, and grandparents all play an active role.

My parents moved to Italy a few years back, so they are full-time grandparents and are a daily part of our children's lives.

Community feels different here

People all seem to know one another where I live in Italy. It's on a different level than the neighborhood I grew up in while in New York City.

Small communities, like the one I live in, tend to be close-knit, with a shared sense of responsibility for looking out for children. That's not something I ever felt when I lived in the US.

Families stay close

In America, there is a strong emphasis on children eventually leaving the nest, the idea that once you're ready for college, you're expected to leave home and become independent.

In Italy, that's simply not the case. Parents seem less focused on their children getting into a prestigious university, securing a stable job, and moving out as quickly as possible. Many Italian children continue living at home until they get married, and that's considered perfectly normal. Heck, even once they get married, many young couples continue to live at home until they eventually have stable jobs and income. It's not looked at in a bad way, but rather that family is always a part of who you are here, no matter your age.

Time will tell how our family will negotiate this stage of life. My parents are a regular part of our lives, and I hope they will continue to be as my children grow.

I'm raising my kids with strategies from both places

I find myself trying to take the best from both worlds while raising my kids. I'm combining independence, family values, and the sense of community I see in Italy, but with that extra emphasis on safety that shaped my upbringing in America.

One thing I personally struggle with is feeling like an American parent while raising my children in Italy. I guess part of me will always be in limbo. No matter where you live in the world, there is no right or wrong way to parent; it's probably hard wherever you call home.

Trump’s failed Fed takeover
Vox - All by Cameron Peters / Sep 16, 2026 at 7:06 PM
1 Company (Federal Reserve)
See Insights Card

This story appeared in The Logoff, a daily newsletter that helps you stay informed about the Trump administration without letting political news take over your life. Subscribe here.

Welcome to The Logoff: President Donald Trump’s Fed gambit has officially failed. 

What happened? On Wednesday, the Federal Reserve voted to raise interest rates by a quarter of a percent, citing ongoing inflation. The rate hike was widely expected, and normally it would be largely unremarkable (at least for this newsletter, which doesn’t generally cover monetary policy). 

In this case, however, it’s also a direct rebuke to Trump, who has spent the last year-and-a-half-plus demanding rate cuts — and plotting a Fed takeover to get them. 

How has Trump gone after the Fed? It’s a long list, but the two most significant episodes are Trump’s efforts to force Fed governor Lisa Cook from her seat to stack the Fed board in his favor (still ongoing) and the Justice Department’s coercive criminal investigation against former Fed chair Jerome Powell (since ended). 

Neither succeeded, but Trump likely thought his goal was in reach when the Senate confirmed his nominee for Fed chair, Kevin Warsh, in May. On Wednesday, however, Warsh — several months into a four-year term as Fed chair — bucked Trump’s persistent demands by voting for the rate hike.

Why does this matter? Trump successfully exerting political control over the Fed would be extremely bad news for just about everyone. Lowering interest rates is a great way to juice the economy in the short term — exactly what he might want as his party faces down a potential midterm wipeout. But it could seriously damage the economy’s long-term health too, which is why the Fed is designed — successfully, as it turns out — to be insulated from partisan politics. 

What else should I know? So far, Trump’s response to the hike has been muted, at least by his standards. 

He’s threatened to take more drastic action, however: Earlier this month, he said he would suspend trade relations “WITH COUNTRIES WITH WHICH WE HAVE A DEFICIT” if the Fed went through with its rate hike.

It’s not clear whether he’ll make good on that threat — but one way or another, we likely haven’t heard the last of Trump’s Fed vendetta.

And with that, it’s time to log off…

Here’s a small bit of good news from New York: After an announcement by Mayor Zohran Mamdani earlier this year, the city is rolling out new, free public restrooms this week as part of a pilot program to address a longstanding complaint by New Yorkers. 

Have a great evening and we’ll see you back here tomorrow!

A detailed look at the '60 Minutes' ratings drop shows the problem facing Bari Weiss
60 Minutes ratings slump
•
Business Insider by jfaris@businessinsider.com (James Faris) / Sep 16, 2026 at 7:04 PM
3 Companies
  • The season debut of "60 Minutes" saw a 20% drop in viewership from last year's premiere.
  • Did viewers not stick around, or did they not watch in the first place?
  • Detailed viewership data offers clues about the issues facing "60 Minutes" and CBS News boss Bari Weiss.

What does the drop in viewers for the "60 Minutes" season premiere mean for CBS News' top editor, Bari Weiss? A look at the granular ratings data provides some clues.

Viewership for the first "60 Minutes" episode led by new producer Nick Bilton, who was handpicked by Weiss in May, fell 20% from 2025's season debut. An average of just under 8 million people watched the newscast, versus over 10 million last September.

The question: Did people start watching and then give up, or did they simply not switch the program on in the first place?

Data from Nielsen shows it was the latter. "60 Minutes" retained its audience for Sunday's opener at a rate similar to last fall's debut, according to data obtained by Business Insider.

The audience for Sunday's broadcast shrank 7.6% after the first 15 minutes, compared to 17% for last year's season debut. Each premiere lost about 47% of its audience from start to finish, the data shows.

Bilton's debut episode featured interviews with the US Air Force pilot who got shot down in Iran, Los Angeles Rams head coach Sean McVay, and the "pardon brokers" who persuade wealthy clients to hire them based on a supposed skill at securing presidential pardons.

It's common for TV shows to lose viewers over the course of an episode, especially news programs like "60 Minutes."

The takeaway for Weiss and crew: The audience didn't revolt after sampling the new-look season. The problem is that they didn't want to watch it.

So, what's going on?

There are a few potential factors, and the ratings for the rest of the season will give the media world more insight.

First, "60 Minutes" has undergone high-profile — and high-drama — changes under Bilton and Weiss, from new graphics to the dramatic departure of four correspondents: Scott Pelley, Cecilia Vega, Sharyn Alfonsi, and Anderson Cooper.

CBS fired Pelley, who grilled his new boss in front of his colleagues about his qualifications, personnel moves, and what he described as Weiss' "murdering" the "60 Minutes" program.

Several former CBS News and "60 Minutes" staffers have accused Weiss of being politically biased, which the former New York Times editor has denied.

One theory is that audiences stayed away because of the controversies.

Then there's the other big issue: The NFL.

The NFL can assist "60 Minutes" by providing a lead-in from the Sunday afternoon games, though the boost wasn't as strong this year.

Viewership for the NFL window leading into Sunday's "60 Minutes" debut, featuring the Minnesota Vikings vs the Green Bay Packers for most of the country, was down over 20% from the comparable game last year — a marquee matchup between the Baltimore Ravens and Patrick Mahomes-led Kansas City Chiefs.

CBS highlighted viewership of "60 Minutes" clips online and across social media, which it said generated about three times the reach of last year's premiere.

The rest of the "60 Minutes" season will show if Weiss and Bilton can claw back those TV viewers, or whether their problems are a lot bigger than the NFL.

Como escolher o melhor tablet para crianças em 2026?

A escolha depende principalmente da idade da criança e do tipo de atividade que será realizada no aparelho. Entre os pontos mais importantes, estão:

  • Idade e proposta: modelos infantis dedicados priorizam proteção física, controle parental e conteúdos direcionados a crianças pequenas; tablets convencionais são mais adequados para estudos e uso prolongado;
  • Tela: considere o tamanho e a resolução de acordo com o uso. Telas de 7 e 8 polegadas facilitam o manuseio por crianças menores, enquanto modelos de 10 ou 11 polegadas oferecem mais espaço para vídeos, leitura e atividades escolares;
  • Desempenho: processador e RAM devem ser avaliados em conjunto. Configurações mais básicas atendem a vídeos, aplicativos educativos e jogos leves, enquanto modelos mais avançados oferecem maior margem para multitarefa e jogos;
  • Armazenamento: 64 GB pode atender a um uso infantil mais simples, mas capacidades maiores são interessantes para guardar jogos, vídeos, fotos e materiais escolares. O suporte a microSD também pode ampliar o espaço disponível;
  • Controle parental: verifique se o tablet oferece ferramentas para limitar tempo de uso, controlar aplicativos e restringir conteúdos. Em modelos convencionais, esses recursos podem ser configurados pelo sistema ou por aplicativos específicos;
  • Resistência e proteção: em tablets infantis, vale observar a presença de cases protetoras e, nos modelos convencionais, certificações como IP68 quando disponíveis;
  • Acessórios: para crianças maiores, suporte a caneta e teclado pode ampliar as possibilidades para desenho, anotações e estudos. É importante verificar se os acessórios acompanham o aparelho ou se são vendidos separadamente.

Qual é o melhor tablet infantil para criança pequena?

Os modelos com temática infantil (Barbie, Peppa Pig, Patrulha Caninca e Minions) fazem mais sentido quando a prioridade é controle parental e acesso a conteúdos apropriados à idade. Além disso, os modelos oferecem cases mais resistentes e até bolsa para transporte.

Qual é o melhor tablet para crianças acima de 10 anos?

Um tablet convencional pode oferecer mais possibilidades para desenho, leitura e entretenimento para crianças maiores. O Lenovo Idea Tab e o Galaxy Tab S10 FE são bons tablets para estudos, pois acrescentam suporte a caneta e recursos de produtividade.

Vale a pena comprar tablet infantil em 2026?

Sim, dependendo da idade e do uso. Tablets infantis fazem sentido principalmente para crianças pequenas que utilizarão o aparelho para vídeos, jogos leves e aplicativos educativos, já que costumam combinar cases protetoras com ferramentas de controle parental.

Para crianças maiores, que também precisam estudar, desenhar ou utilizar aplicativos mais variados, um tablet convencional com recursos de controle parental pode oferecer mais possibilidades no dia a dia.

Qual marca tem os melhores tablets infantis?

Amazon, Multilaser e Positivo oferecem modelos com proposta especificamente infantil ou com temática voltada às crianças.

A Multilaser possui versões licenciadas de personagens como Barbie, Peppa Pig e Patrulha Canina, enquanto a Positivo oferece o Vision Tab 7 em versões com personagens como Minions. A Amazon, por sua vez, possui a linha Fire Kids, desenvolvida com recursos específicos de controle e proteção para o público infantil.

Qual é a diferença entre um tablet comum e tablet infantil?

A diferença não está apenas no tamanho ou no visual: ela também envolve a proposta de uso, as especificações técnicas e o custo-benefício de um tablet.

O modelo infantil é desenvolvido considerando necessidades específicas de crianças, normalmente combinando case protetora, controles parentais e interface ou conteúdos direcionados ao público infantil.

Já um tablet comum prioriza uma utilização mais ampla e pode oferecer hardware mais potente, aplicativos e recursos de produtividade.
